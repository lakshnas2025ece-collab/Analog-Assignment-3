1. Introduction

An oscillator converts DC power into a periodic AC signal without any external periodic input. A feedback oscillator is an amplifier (gain A) whose output is fed back through a frequency-selective network (feedback factor β) so that the returned signal reinforces the input. The Barkhausen criterion gives the conditions on the loop gain Aβ under which a feedback system can sustain a sinusoid at a steady amplitude.

Barkhausen criterion: at the oscillation frequency f₀, (i) magnitude |Aβ| = 1, and (ii) phase ∠Aβ = 0° (or n·360°).

Key terms

  • Positive (regenerative) feedback: the net feedback signal adds in phase with the input, so the total loop phase shift is 0°/360°.
  • Amplifier gain A: open-loop (no-feedback) gain, dimensionless (or dB).
  • Feedback factor β: fraction of output returned, β = Vf/Vo.
  • Loop gain Aβ: gain around the complete loop with the loop broken at one point.
  • Startup: |Aβ| slightly > 1 so noise grows. Steady state: amplitude limited so effective |Aβ| = 1.

Prerequisites: feedback amplifiers, op-amp gain formulas, RC phase shift, resonance of LC circuits, complex impedance.

Classification

TypeExamplesTypical range
RC (frequency from R, C)Wien bridge, RC phase shiftHz – ~1 MHz (audio)

The Barkhausen criterion applies to all of them: each uses an amplifier plus a frequency-selective feedback network (RC network, LC tank, or crystal) that satisfies the phase condition at only one frequency.

3. Working Principle

  1. Noise: thermal/switch-on transients contain components at all frequencies, including f₀.
  2. Selection: only at f₀ does the feedback network return the signal in phase (loop phase 0°/360°).
  3. Amplification: the amplifier boosts the component; the network feeds back a fraction β.
  4. Growth: if |Aβ| > 1 the returned signal exceeds the original, so amplitude builds up each pass.
  5. Limiting: supply rails or a stabilizer reduce the effective gain until the average |Aβ| = 1 and the amplitude is steady.
Loop gainBehaviour
|Aβ| < 1 (phase 0°)Each pass is smaller: oscillation decays.
|Aβ| = 1Ideal sustained oscillation at constant amplitude (marginal).
|Aβ| > 1Amplitude grows during startup until nonlinearity/amplitude control reduces the effective gain to 1.
Ideal vs. practical: |Aβ| = 1 exactly is unstable in practice (component tolerance, temperature and ageing drift it either way). Real oscillators are designed with |Aβ| slightly > 1 at startup, plus an amplitude stabilizer. Barkhausen is a necessary condition; it is not sufficient (it does not guarantee startup, stable amplitude, or low distortion).

Common misconceptions

  • "Aβ = 1 at startup" – wrong; startup needs > 1.
  • "Feedback must be positive in sign" – what matters is total loop phase 0°/360°, e.g. 180° amplifier + 180° network.
  • "An external signal is needed" – only noise is needed.
  • Phase of 360° ≡ 0°.

Advantages: simple design rule, applies to every feedback oscillator. Limitations: linear analysis only; ignores amplitude limiting, loading, and tolerance.

5. Multisim Simulation Guide

  1. Construct: place a 741/LM324 (or virtual op-amp) and build the Wien bridge per the connection list. Example: R₁ = R₂ = 10 kΩ, C₁ = C₂ = 16 nF → f₀ ≈ 995 Hz.
  2. Gain network: Rg = 10 kΩ, Rf ≈ 20.5–21 kΩ (use a potentiometer) giving A slightly above 3; add two anti-parallel diodes across part of Rf for amplitude control.
  3. Power: ±12 V DC sources, with a common ground.
  4. Transient analysis: Simulate → Analyses → Transient; end time ≈ 50 ms; tick "Set initial conditions" to Calculate DC operating point / user-defined with a small nonzero node voltage if needed to kick-start.
  5. Run & observe: view Vo on the Grapher or oscilloscope; expect growth then constant amplitude.
  6. Frequency: use cursors to measure one period T; f = 1/T. Compare with 1/(2πRC).
  7. Verify Barkhausen: break the loop at the + input, drive with an AC source, and use AC Analysis (Bode plotter): check |Aβ| ≈ 0 dB and phase ≈ 0° at f₀.
  8. Troubleshoot: no oscillation → increase Rf; clipped/distorted → reduce Rf or add limiter; wrong frequency → check R and C values; flat output → add initial condition/noise.

6. Equations and Derivations

A. Feedback and loop gain

For positive feedback: Vo = A(Vi + βVo) ⇒

Af = Vo/Vi = A / (1 − Aβ)

Characteristic equation: 1 − Aβ(jω) = 0. For a nonzero Vo with Vi = 0, the denominator must vanish, so Aβ = 1 + j0:

|Aβ| = 1    and    ∠Aβ = 0°, ±360°, …

Limiting cases: Aβ < 1 → finite gain, decays; Aβ → 1 → Af → ∞. Equivalent: amplifier gain |A| = 1/|β| at f₀. In decibels: 20 log|A| + 20 log|β| = 0 dB. Variables are dimensionless (gain in dB).

B. RC phase-shift oscillator (3 equal sections)

f₀ = 1/(2πRC√6)    |β| = 1/29    |A|min = 29 (inverting, 180°)

Each RC section gives up to 90°; three sections give 180° at the frequency where the network phase is 180°, which together with the inverting amplifier gives 360°. (Assumption: ideal, with the last R accounted for in the network; real amplifiers loading the network change the factor 29.)

C. Wien bridge (equal R, C)

β(jω) = Z₂/(Z₁+Z₂), with Z₁ = R + 1/jωC, Z₂ = R/(1+jωRC). This simplifies to

β = 1 / [3 + j(ωRC − 1/ωRC)]

The imaginary part is zero when ωRC = 1 ⇒

f₀ = 1/(2πRC),   β = 1/3,   A = 1 + Rf/Rg = 3 ⇒ Rf = 2Rg

D. LC oscillators

Colpitts: f₀ = 1/(2π√(L·Ceq)), Ceq = C₁C₂/(C₁+C₂)
Hartley: f₀ = 1/(2π√(Leq·C)), Leq = L₁ + L₂ + 2M

The inductor and capacitor exchange energy at resonance; the tank sets the frequency and the amplifier replenishes losses. For a BJT-type Colpitts / Hartley, the requirement is approximately hfe ≥ C₂/C₁ (Colpitts) and L₁/L₂ (Hartley), subject to the transistor configuration and loading.

E. Gain equations

A = Vo/Vi;   AdB = 20 log₁₀|A|;   β = Vf/Vo;   (Aβ)dB = AdB + βdB = 0 dB

7. Solved Problems

Example 1 (basic) – Can this oscillate?

An inverting amplifier has A = −50 and the feedback network gives β = −0.02 at f₀. Find: Aβ, and whether sustained oscillation is possible.

Aβ = (−50)(−0.02) = +1.0 ⇒ |Aβ| = 1, phase 0° (the two 180° shifts add to 360°).

Answer: Barkhausen conditions are satisfied. In practice use a slightly larger gain for startup.

Example 2 – Frequency of a Wien bridge

R = 10 kΩ, C = 16 nF. f₀ = 1/(2πRC) = 1/(2π × 10×10³ × 16×10⁻⁹) = 1/(1.005×10⁻³) ≈ 995 Hz. Required gain = 3 ⇒ Rf = 2Rg.

Example 3 (GATE-style) – RC phase shift design

Three-section RC oscillator, R = 1 kΩ, C = 100 nF. f₀ = 1/(2π × 10³ × 10⁻⁷ × 2.449) = 1/(1.539×10⁻³) ≈ 650 Hz. With an inverting op-amp whose input resistor equals R = 1 kΩ, Rf ≥ 29R = 29 kΩ.

Shortcut: f₀ = 0.0650/RC for the three-section phase-shift network (RC product in seconds); 0.065/10⁻⁴ = 650 Hz ✓.

Example 4 (LC) – Colpitts frequency

L = 1 mH, C₁ = C₂ = 2 nF ⇒ Ceq = 1 nF. f₀ = 1/(2π√(10⁻³ × 10⁻⁹)) = 1/(2π × 10⁻⁶) ≈ 159 kHz.

9. Interview Preparation

Industry perspective: In practice engineers rarely build oscillators from Barkhausen alone: they run loop-gain (stb/AC) analysis, check startup margin (typically 2–3× gain), temperature and process corners, and phase noise. Crystal oscillators are verified for negative resistance and drive level.

10. Applications and Industry Relevance

  • Clock generation: crystal oscillators for microcontrollers, FPGAs and SoCs.
  • Communication: local oscillators (LC/VCO) in RF transmitters and receivers.
  • Signal generators: Wien bridge audio generators, test equipment.
  • Instrumentation, timers, audio tone generators.

Challenges: startup failure, amplitude drift, temperature stability, phase noise, parasitic capacitance. Roles: analog/RF design, hardware validation, embedded hardware, test engineering. Barkhausen lets engineers design the loop, estimate frequency, and diagnose "no oscillation" (loop gain too low or phase wrong) or "distortion" (gain too high).

Comparisons

ItemCompared
Amplifier vs oscillatorAmplifier needs an input; oscillator needs none, uses positive feedback and a frequency-selective network.
Positive vs negative feedbackPositive (loop phase 0°) increases gain/instability – used in oscillators; negative reduces gain, stabilizes and widens bandwidth.
RC vs LCRC: low frequencies (audio), compact, tunable; LC: radio frequency, higher Q.
Wien vs RC phase shiftWien: gain 3, β = 1/3, non-inverting, tunable with a dual pot. Phase shift: gain 29, inverting, harder to tune.
Startup vs steady stateStartup: |Aβ| > 1, amplitude growing. Steady state: effective |Aβ| = 1, amplitude constant.

Troubleshooting

No oscillation: gain low, phase error, loading, or no kick-start. Clipping: gain too high. Frequency drift: poor component stability – use a crystal.

Memory aid: Wien = "3 and 1/3"; phase shift = "29, 1/29, √6".
